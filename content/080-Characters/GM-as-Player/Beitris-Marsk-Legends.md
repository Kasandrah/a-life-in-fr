# Legends

For Taevyn, Rose, Killy, and Max.

## The Blade of Justice

_Restov, a dueling salle of the Aldori, wine and old wounds_

---

"You hold your guard too high."

The young student flushed. "Master Korrivan says—"

"Korrivan never saw the Blade of Justice fight." The old swordlord settled into his chair, the wine dark in his cup. "I did. Once. In Mivon, before he vanished."

The students exchanged glances. They had heard the name. A ghost story for duelists. A cautionary tale, or an aspiration, depending on who told it.

"He came from the Moonsea. That much is certain. Some say he was a noble's ward. Some say a freedom fighter's son. Some say _both_—that he walked between worlds, knew fathers in one life who were strangers in another." The old man shrugged. "The Moonsea was strange in those days. The Witch Queen's time. Everything was strange."

"But his bladework—"

"Ah." The swordlord's eyes went distant. "His bladework."

He set down the cup.

"You know how we fight. Clean. Efficient. The Aldori way is to end a duel in one stroke, maybe two. Economy of motion. No wasted effort."

The students nodded.

"He was _nothing_ like that."

The old man laughed at their expressions.

"He would tumble. Cartwheel. Spin his opponent like a dancing partner and open their throat on the return. Completely unnecessary. Utterly inefficient. And absolutely _untouchable_." He traced a scar along his forearm. "I lasted eight seconds. Felt like he toyed with me for an hour. When I hit the stones, he was already striking a pose. A _pose_. Rapier raised, other hand flourished behind him, like a player taking a bow."

"That sounds like mockery."

"That's what I thought. So I asked him, after. When he helped me up." The swordlord's voice went quiet. "He said he'd spent his whole life training for battles that mattered. That when the fighting was real—when friends died, when the world hung in the balance—you didn't get to be pretty. You just survived. So when he _could_ dance, when the stakes were low and no one he loved was bleeding out..."

He picked up his wine again.

"He danced. Because someday he wouldn't be able to."

---

Silence in the salle.

"Where did he go?" the young student asked.

"No one knows. Some say he went looking for his father. Some say he'd made a bargain—the wrong kind—and had to pay it off somewhere far from here. Some say he's still out there, teaching lordlings to hold their guard too high and waiting for another world to need saving."

The old swordlord drained his cup.

"But I'll tell you what I believe. I believe he found whatever he was looking for. And I believe that wherever he is, if you challenged him tomorrow, he'd still spin you like a ballroom partner and leave you wondering why you ever picked up a blade."

He stood, joints creaking.

"Now. Your guard. Lower it. And _move_ when you parry—he taught me that much, at least. The Aldori way isn't the only way."

The students raised their blades.

Somewhere, perhaps, a young man who was once a boy who never knew his father smiled at nothing in particular, and danced.

## The Red Lady

_A chapel of Ragathiel, somewhere in the Moonsea, the Feast of Burning Swords_

---

The novitiate polished the shrine in silence, as she did every evening. The candles flickered against the red-lacquered armour displayed behind the altar—too small for most knights, sized for a woman of modest height and immoderate conviction.

"You have questions."

She startled. The senior templar had entered without a sound, as he always did.

"The armour," she admitted. "The records say it belonged to a cleric of Lathander. But this is a chapel of Ragathiel."

"It belonged to both." The templar settled onto a pew, his own armour creaking. "She came to the Morninglord first. Believed in redemption. Second chances. The dawn always comes."

"What changed?"

"The Moonsea." He said it like a diagnosis. "She travelled with a witch who gave her eye to Gruumsh. Watched a friend become a ghost and had to banish him herself. Fought devils and demons and men who were worse than either. Somewhere along the way, she stopped believing the dawn was enough."

The novitiate's hand stilled on the shrine.

"She came to Ragathiel because she understood something most paladins never learn. Mercy is sacred. But so is _judgment_." The templar's voice was quiet, almost gentle. "She held the Dagger—one of the Cards, the old weapons. It showed her where evil hid. And she went there. Every time. No matter what it cost."

"The stories say she killed a version of herself."

"A dark reflection. A woman named Tris Marsk, who had made different choices. Wore Norgorber's black where Rose wore Ragathiel's red." The templar shrugged. "Some say she hesitated. Some say she didn't. The Church doesn't record which, and I think that's intentional."

He rose, joints protesting.

"She was canonised sixty years ago. The youngest saint in Ragathiel's communion. Not because she was merciful. Not because she was kind. Because she understood that sometimes love demands you become the blade."

The novitiate looked at the armour. At the notches along the vambraces. At the scorch marks that no amount of polish would remove.

"What happened to her?"

"She finished her work." The templar moved toward the door. "Freed the Moonsea. Broke the Zhentarim's back. Walked into Hillsfar beside the Witch Queen and walked out carrying Elven children who'd never seen the sun."

"And then?"

"And then she put down her sword." He paused at the threshold. "Founded a hospital in Melvaunt. Spent her last thirty years healing instead of hurting. Died in her sleep, surrounded by people she'd saved."

The candles flickered.

"That's the part they don't tell you about Ragathiel's saints. The blade is only sacred if you know when to sheathe it."

He left her alone with the armour and the silence and the weight of a life measured in wounds—given and healed alike.

## The Ironhide

_Somewhere in the gladiatorial circuits, a veteran speaks to a contender_

---

"You want to fight like the Ironhide, you gotta understand something first."

The young gladiator rolled his shoulders, all muscle and impatience. "I understand plenty. Biggest. Strongest. Last one standing."

"That's what everyone thinks." The old pit-fighter laughed, a sound like gravel in a drum. "That's what _I_ thought, before I saw him."

He traced the ruin of his left ear.

"Melvaunt. The Liberation Games. They held them every year after the Moonsea treaties, celebration of freedom and all that. I was young. Thought I was the best in three cities. Walked into that arena and saw a mountain."

"He was that big?"

"Bigger than big. But that wasn't what made him dangerous." The old fighter leaned close. "I'd fought big men. Strong men. Killed a few. What made Ironhide different was that he _knew_ what he was."

"What does that mean?"

"It means he never pretended. Never postured. Never roared or beat his chest or tried to make you afraid." The old man's eyes went distant. "He just looked at you with those steady eyes, raised that orcish axe—and you understood that whatever happened next, he had already accepted it. Win or lose. Live or die. He'd made his peace."

The young gladiator shifted, uncomfortable.

"I lasted two exchanges. He could have killed me on the first. Opened me up, spilled my guts for the crowd. Instead he took my ear, stepped back, and _waited_. Let me yield."

"That's mercy."

"No. That's _respect_." The old fighter shook his head. "Mercy is what you give to the weak. Respect is what you give to someone worth fighting. He gave me the choice to walk away. Most wouldn't have."

"Where is he now?"

"No one knows. After the Liberation, he stopped fighting in the pits. Some say he went back to the Moonsea, helped rebuild. Some say he found a woman, settled down, raised children who never had to fight for anyone's entertainment." The old man smiled. "Some say he's still out there, waiting for a battle worth his time."

He clapped the young gladiator on the shoulder.

"You want to fight like the Ironhide? Stop trying to be the strongest. Start trying to be the kind of man who knows when strength matters and when it doesn't."

"That sounds like philosophy."

"That's because it is." The old fighter turned away. "The arena teaches you to kill. The Ironhide taught me when _not_ to. That's worth more than any victory."

## The Last Verse

_A tavern in Waterdeep, centuries hence, a bard tunes her lute_

---

"Play us the Handy Cycle!"

The bard smiled, fingers dancing across the strings. "Which part? The man wrote three hundred songs, nineteen plays, and a comic opera about a talking fish."

"The Ironride Ballad!"

"The Witch Queen's Lament!"

"The one about the spiders!"

She laughed and let the chord ring.

"You know the thing about Maximilian Handy? Nobody agrees on what he looked like. The paintings all contradict each other. Tall in one, short in another. Handsome, plain, scarred, unblemished. Some say he changed his face with magic. Some say every portrait was a different lover's memory."

The tavern quieted.

"What everyone agrees on is this: he was _there_. Every impossible moment in the Moonsea liberation? Handy was there. The Ironride crash. The Witch Queen's transformation. The Red Lady's duel with her shadow. The fall of Hillsfar."

She played a minor chord, then a major.

"He didn't fight like the others. Wasn't strong like the Ironhide, holy like the Red Lady, wild like the Witch Queen, quick like the Blade. He was just... present. Watching. Taking notes."

"Sounds like a coward."

"Sounds like a _witness_." The bard's eyes glinted. "Someone had to remember. Someone had to make sure the truth survived. That's what bards _do_."

She struck up a melody—something old, something half-forgotten, something that made the older patrons' eyes go distant.

"He died in a temple of Norgorber. Spiders, of all things. Poison in the dark." The melody went minor. "But death didn't stop Maximilian Handy. He came back. Unfinished business, you see. Songs unsung. Stories untold."

The bard's voice dropped.

"The Red Lady drove him off. Had to. She'd done it before, to another friend who wouldn't stay dead. Couldn't abide the undead, not even ones she loved. They say she wept when she spoke the words. They say he bowed—one last performance—before he faded."

Silence in the tavern.

"But here's the thing." The bard struck a chord, bright and defiant. "Before she banished him? He _finished_. Every song. Every verse. Spent his ghostly months making sure it was all written down. The Ironride Ballad, the Witch Queen's Lament, the Blade's Dance, the Ironhide's Vigil. All of it."

She grinned.

"That's Maxwell Handy. Died twice and still met his deadlines."

Laughter, a little uncertain.

"Every time you sing his songs, every time you tell the story of the Witch Queen, every time some child asks about the Red Lady—that's him. Still performing. Still taking his bow. The Red Lady sent him on, but she couldn't banish _this_."

She gestured around the tavern. At the listeners. At the air thick with old stories.

"He's here. In every verse. And that's the only immortality a bard ever really wanted."

The melody swelled, and the tavern sang along to words penned by a dead man who had refused—twice—to leave the stage before his work was done.
