# Lord Rhodri

## Gwrfelling

Gwrfelling, Rhodri's father, was a knight who wandered from lord to lord as the need for fighters rose and fell in an area.  By most knights' standards, he was not all that successful.  He failed to become a lord.  He failed to get his own lands.  He even failed to have a large retinue.  In fact, he failed to have a retinue.  And above all, he failed to stay with his lord, or (in fact) any lord at all.  Surely the signs of a failed mercenary knight.

However, Gwrfelling considered himself very successful.  He lacked nothing.  If he needed a horse, his lord (whomever that might be at the time) provided a fine charger of noble mien, stout heart and fiery disport to everyone but his master.  If he needed to be entertained, he had all the prerequisite skills and abilities, propensity, and wit to find some delectation to fascinate him.  Above all, he did not need more than he could carry on a horse to find self-respect.

Also, he found that staying in one place too long led to feelings of dissatisfaction and disgust.  These feelings came when long periods of the pressures of conflict waned; when he saw how low his beloved Britannia, luminary of the Empire, had stooped.  All the glory that was Britannia, hardened within the forge of the Empire, was fading, ... fading away.  Could no-one stop this?  Could no-one see this?  Could no-one but him?

After travelling for a while, he would find a lord who seemed different.  A lord who looked different from the others; someone who remembered what it was to be part of the Empire, to be part of a noble culture which showed the beauty within humanity.  Surely this was worth fighting for.

And fight he did.  Long and hard.  Until there were no more wild, uncouth barbarians to fight.  ....Or until the shining light of Imperial Rome was still within the eyes while the spilt blood cooled and the spirit stole away.  Although the latter was more tragic, Gwrfelling feared the former.  For each lord who was victorious, anger and betrayal crept in as the notions of Rome and the ideals they had espoused were forgotten or spurned.  In a little time, with no pressure of conflict to cloud the vision, that the barbarians had been defeated lay more and more open to question.

If they could not espouse the cause of keeping the glory of the Empire alive, then Gwrfelling could.  He would try to reason with them as a cultured Imperial would have no trouble doing, but he could not succeed.

## Mithraism

Initiation into the cult of Mithras was gained only after undergoing considerable ordeals.  This was a cult from which women were barred, and which appealed particularly to soldiers; it was Persian in origin, but through contact with Mesopotamia, it assimilated a strong astrological background.

During the Hellenistic period this cult spread widely in Asia Minor, and it was said by Plutarch to have been brought to the West by the Cilician pirates whom Pompey later exterminated; if this was so, the cult must have been very much confined to the lowest classes living in the great ports of Italy, among whom in fact it flourished for a long time.

Mithraism was popular over a long period: it appears first to have become much practised under the Flavians, and the cult was still widespread in the 4th century AD, by which time Mithras had been identified with the Invincible Sun god.  From the middle of the 1st Century AD onwards worship of Mithras spread with the occupying forces of the Roman armies, up the Danube and down the Rhone and the Rhine, and as far afield as Britain, where a Mithraeum was found in Walbrook in the City of London.

Mithras was usually worshipped in a small sanctuary constructed to look like the underground cave in which, according to tradition, he had been born.  There were seven grades of initiates, each of which was associated with one of the planets.  The lower grades, known together as the Servitors, were Raven, Bridegroom, and Soldier, while the upper grades called collectively Participants, were Lion, Persian, Courier of the Sun, and Father.

The ordeals the initiates had first to undergo might be of great severity, for in addition to fasting there were ordeals by scourging, branding, and exposure to extreme heat and cold, and they may sometimes have involved human sacrifice.  A magical papyrus from Egypt of the early 3rd Century AD seems to preserve part of the liturgy for initiation into the highest grade of the cult, which consists of invocations and prayers for a spiritual rebirth.

In the unending war of good against evil, Mithras was a good spirit, an attendant on Ahuramazda, the Lord of Life.  He was also regarded as an agent of the Invincible Sun, with whom he was later identified.  In fact he is sometimes shown, wearing the characteristic Phrygian cap, in the company of the Sun.

Mithras’s most important exploit was believed to have been the killing of a great bull; the first of living creatures, from whose blood sprang corn and all other life.  Ahriman, the power of darkness, sent plagues and flames to try to overwhelm this new life, but Mithras overcame them, and so, having accomplished his mission on earth, he ascended to heaven in the chariot of the Sun.

However, before the ascent to heaven Mithras and the Sun shared a sacred meal, which was afterwards re-enacted periodically by the followers of Mithras; it has been conjectured that initiates with the rank of Courier of the Sun and Father presided at these feasts, or communion services, as they have been called.  It is hardly surprising that Christian writers took these feasts, as well, as parodies of the Eucharist.
