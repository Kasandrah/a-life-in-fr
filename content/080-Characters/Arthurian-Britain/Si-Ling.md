# Si Ling

Si Ling is the legendary Phoenix, a fabulous and mythical creature and the only one of its kind.  The Phoenix is a part of Hindu, Chinese, Greek, Arabian, and Egyptian mythology.  It has also appeared in medieval Christian writings as a symbol of death and resurrection.

## Background

### In Western Countries

Herodotus’ Heliopolitan Guides describe the Phoenix as resembling an eagle in size and shape, and as appearing in Egypt only once every five hundred to a thousand years.  It lived in Arabia and was sacred to, or a servant of, the Sun God of ancient Egypt.  The phoenix has also been described as a heron, but is usually depicted as a peacock or eagle-like bird with red and gold plumage.

At the close of its life, the Phoenix would fly to Arabia, build a nest of spices, sing a melodious dirge, flap its wings to set fire to the nest and burn itself to ashes.  Then the Phoenix would arise from the funeral pyre, reborn and embalm the ashes of its predecessor in an egg of myrrh.

The new Phoenix would fly back to the Temple of Heliopolis in the City of the Sun and place the myrrh egg on the altar of the Sun God.  The priests would worship the Phoenix again until it returned to Arabia to repeat the cycle once more.

This process of death and rebirth is said to symbolise the setting and rising of the sun.  The phoenix symbolises immortality, resurrection, and life after death.  Its flight is said to represent the capacity to leave the world and its problems behind, flying towards the sun in pure, clear skies.

### In China

Images of the Phoenix (si ling) have appeared in China for over 7,000 years, often in jade and originally on good luck totems.  The Phoenix is commonly depicted attacking snakes with its talons, wings spread.

The Phoenix was also used to depict the Empress, usually paired with a dragon depicting the Emperor.  In this instance, the Phoenix represented power sent from the heavens to the Empress.  During the Han period, 2,200 years ago, a male and female phoenix facing each other were used to depict the direction south.

If a phoenix was used to decorate a house, it symbolised the loyalty and honesty of the people living there.  It was also believed that the Phoenix controlled the five tones of Chinese music and that it represented the Confucian virtues of loyalty, honesty, decorum, and justice.  Depictions of the Phoenix were placed on tombs and graves.  Phoenix jewellery was used to show high moral values and was only worn by people of importance.
