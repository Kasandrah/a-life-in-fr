## Appearance

David’s outward appearance is of a college graduate dressed in a business suit.

His short unruly blonde hair is barely controlled by his barber and his appealing, boyish good looks make him appear much younger than his actual age.  His vivid blue eyes and tall, strong build complete his Germanic appearance, a great advantage in his wartime role as a intelligence officer.

Although he prefers a more relaxed style of dress, David’s business commitments and natural good taste in clothes make expensive hand tailored suits and hand made shoes his usual attire.
## Interests

David is not as dedicated a party goer as Diana.  Having had more than enough excitement during the war, he now prefers quieter pursuits.  He loves communication, languages and books and is a voracious reader.  He speaks four languages in addition to English; German as a second language; French and Russian quite well and Latin.  David is an excellent bridge player and an elegant dancer.  Those who know him well are aware of his strong interest in the occult.

Most of his time is consumed by his business activities as a highly successful industrialist and the head of Hasalt Associates.  His accounting and business administration skills serve him well in this role.

During the war David developed skills in first aid, sailing, piloting aircraft, cryptography, radio operation and judo.  He is a crack shot with a pistol and knows how to use a knife.

## Personality

David was initially a brash and carefree young man with a strong desire to travel and explore new places.  However, his wartime experiences dramatically altered his personality.  He is now somewhat withdrawn and quite, preferring the security of people and places he knows well.  He is happy to let others be the centre of attention while he quietly absorbs what is going on around him.  Few people earn his trust, but once earned his support is always guaranteed.  His sense of humour is far more subdued than it was and finds expression in witty remarks and gentle teasing.

David is a quick witted, charming and persuasive communicator, usually winning any argument.  He is insatiably curious, likes to be up on events and dislikes being excluded.  He is quick on the uptake or to seize opportunities and is naturally innovative, traits that serves him well in business.  He uses his learned self-discipline to direct his intelligence and creative potential and follows through what he begins.

## Background

The van Hasalt family, of whom David Garrick van Hasalt is now the head, is an old American one.  They originated in the Netherlands and the first in the New World was Jergold van Hasalt who settled in New Amsterdam in 1631.  He ran a reasonably profitable merchant's business, but fled in 1648 back to the Netherlands after he accidentally killed a neighbour over a disputation concerning the said neighbour's wife.

Over the next century, van Hasalts ebbed and flowed from the New World to the Netherlands, and later to Britain (when they had become more assimilated with the predominantly Anglo-Saxon nature of the colonies).  By 1733, they had settled permanently in New England, descendants of Jergold's younger brother, Willem.  He was a farmer and reasonably wealthy, owning two good properties in New Hampshire and a smaller one in south-west Vermont.

The van Hasalts looked set to become solid and unexceptional members of the American "squirearchy", though once in a while a van Hasalt went to sea.  It is hard to take the sea from the veins of a Netherlander.

Cornelius van Hasalt was a staunch supporter of the War of Independence and he fought with distinction with the rebels, rising to the rank of Colonel and gaining the friendship of General Washington.  Following independence though, Cornelius was content to sink back into relative obscurity, tending the properties at Hampton Beach, New Hampshire.  The Vermont land had been fairly thoroughly destroyed by the British and Hessian troops during the fighting, and the van Hasalts largely abandoned it.

Cornelius was succeeded by his son, also Cornelius, who never married (though siring various offspring, usually called Hassell or Cornelson or similar) and who brought up his late brother's children, George Washington van Hasalt and Marjorie van Hasalt.  George married well but Marjorie married James Gordon, a much older man who was a skilled cabinetmaker and who died in 1856.  All three of their children died without issue and only their youngest, a son, married.  A descendant of James Gordon's sister is Mildred Baines, who works for David van Hasalt as his confidential secretary.

In 1810 Cornelius started to reinvestigate the Vermont property and in so doing discovered silver, not a big find but quite enough to catapult the van Hasalts well up the social ladder of the time.  From mining they diversified into shipping, lumber, engineering and armaments, the last not directly.  They tended to finance munitions manufacturers rather than manufacture themselves.

George Washington van Hasalt was the major diversifier, though he died only nineteen years after the formidable Cornelius relinquished his hold on life.  George built the mansion at Hampton Beach.  He disapproved of the Civil War as more normal profits vanished, but the new opportunities added immeasurably to his wealth.  A dull and colourless man, he was vicious, unprincipled, ruthless and cunning in business, and Hasalt Associates received a foundation from his policies that has kept it stable ever since.

His wife had a miserable life and spent much of it with her relatives in New York in Mohawk Valley.  George didn't care.  His sister Marjorie, who had long been a widow, was killed in 1863 by a stray shell as she nursed troops in the war.  He didn't care about that either.  He cared only for money and his company.

George arranged his son's engagement when his son was still only eighteen.  George chose for his son a young and wealthy girl of illustrious lineage (the New York Beaumonts) but her father lost his wealth in a series of disasters.  George ruthlessly broke the engagement and this shattered Belinda, who suffered a series of nervous breakdowns for the rest of her comparatively short life, and made his son an externally grey man, just like George.

When George Washington van Hasalt II did eventually marry it was the van Hasalt wealth and power to the society wit, sophistication and class of Amanda Franklin-Hall.  Neither loved the other, or ever did, but the marriage was a superb example of a successful business merger, and treated as such.

George's sister Jane went her own way, like their Aunt Marjorie, and married a penniless young Army doctor in 1876.  They moved to the frontier, but in 1883 Michael was captured and slain by the Sioux in one of their periodic uprisings.  At the news Jane had a collapse and miscarried.  She lives in the village of Cordray in New Hampshire subsisting on her minimal war widow's pension and is blissfully happy.  She despises her sister-in-law, Amanda, and if help is ever needed against Amanda, Jane will do whatever she can.

Amanda van Hasalt pinned all her hopes on her firstborn son, George Washington van Hasalt III.  Similarly to persons of earlier generations Amanda was more fiercely a 'van Hasalt' than a born van Hasalt.  When she discovered her son was mentally retarded she blamed herself and her husband for what she had given birth to, and her son for being retarded and breaking all her plans.  She resolved never to hope again.  Consequently, her subsequent children David, Elizabeth and Philadelphia ("Philly") grew up in a loveless environment.

Their father had no personality outside of his work, as he was a less amoral shadow of his own father, and their mother no longer cared about them as children but only as continuers and glorifiers of the van Hasalt name.

David and Philly love each other greatly and each loved their brother George, a loving, kindly, sweet natured boy and young man.  They had no other people to turn to, the servants were not in the job for the affection of their charges.  Tutors and teachers came and went as they clashed with Amanda.  Elizabeth was at first part of the children's group, but as she grew older she took on her mother's personality and desires.  David and Philly took after Cornelius I and Jane respectively, so they were unpopular with Amanda as they were too hard to control.

In 1919 Elizabeth married Captain Andrew Long, a returned Army career officer through whom she felt she could achieve society's acceptance.  She fancied being a general's wife, but Andrew is dedicated and highly honest and devoted to his country's service rather that to himself.  He transferred to the Engineers in order to make the most use of his talents and since then his marriage to Elizabeth has been disintegrating.  Andrew fully expects to be divorced from Elizabeth shortly, though, as he still partly loves Elizabeth and deeply loves his son, the prospect distresses him greatly.  He does not let his anguish show and continues to devote himself to his country and his son.

Philadelphia was married off later in 1919 to Dermot, Lord Kilkenny, an Irish peer.  Amanda had long been trying to get a genuine 'English Lord' in the family.  It was well that Philly and Dermot liked each other at first sight and feel in love rather thoroughly, as Philly would have enraged Amanda immeasurably by refusing to marry anyone who Amanda favoured.  David is very fond of his brothers-in-law.

George Washington van Hasalt III died in 1914, not yet 21, from complications arising from the same fault that caused his mental handicap.  Amanda was relieved at the thought of the final removal of such an 'embarrassment to the family’, and such a reflection on her.  Her husband was actually more upset than he had expected to be, or regrettably was ever able to express to anyone.  It might have altered his relationship with his two most vital offspring, if not with his wife.  Elizabeth followed her mother's line and didn't even attend the funeral.  David and Philly were deeply upset.

In 1916 David joined the Diplomatic Corps as a junior and due to a flair for foreign languages was posted to Berlin.  He was recruited secretly by the United States Military Intelligence Service with the rank of full lieutenant and put under the command of the on-station chief, a chauffeur.  David spent 1916 and early 1917 wandering the country as a German accountant (Freiderich Volksmeister, exempt from military service due to bad sight) or as a translator (Carl Brunner, exempted by his occupation).  He got much information out before the USA entered the war.

At that point he swapped identities with John Cartwright, a compromised agent, and when the diplomatic staff returned to London, David, at the tender age of 21, stayed behind to pass information on.  He did an exceptionally valuable job until March 1918 when he was betrayed, then captured by the Imperial Secret Intelligence Service.  He was questioned by Joachim and Willi von Starnberg, the lethal agents who resembled nothing so much as Proto-Nazis.  He was tortured by their tool, Gunther Krause and never broke, though he bent and cracked extensively.

In September while the Starnbergs and Krause were away supervising the transfer of their effects and assets to Switzerland (they could see the writing on the wall), David escaped with the help of an ugly but cheerful English sympathiser Johann Tannenberg.  David stayed secretly with Johann and his wife Tania until the Armistice, slowly recovering.  It was only while David was in hospital, where he stayed until 1920, that he learned Johann was actually Jack Holland, a British spy.  Johann/Jack had been at large in Germany from 1914 until 1917 when he was captured.  He was aided in escaping later in 1917 by Tania Grossleitner, who was machine-gunned as she helped him.  As a result she lost her legs and Jack spent the rest of the war nursing her and helping the Allied effort as quietly as he could.

David was honourably discharged as a full Colonel, one of the Army's youngest, and awarded the Congressional Medal of Honour, Distinguished Conduct and Service Medals and the Silver Star for his exploits.  He was also ordered not to talk about his experiences.  The Department of Defence then steered various lucrative contracts the way of Hasalt Associates.  Truth to tell, David occasionally does favours for Army Intelligence, but very rarely now.

Out of hospital, those who knew him found him a different man from the brash, carefree youth who had blithely gone to war.  He was withdrawn, nervous and very wary of strangers.  Amanda started showing him signs of seemingly genuine affection, but in actual truth the prospect of a van Hasalt war hero was too much for her sensibilities and she was attempting to exploit him for his PR. value to herself and the family.

David fell into a position of trust with his mother, never dreaming that she would betray him if he no longer filled her purposes.  He threw himself into studies and achieved a brilliant pass in a remarkably brief time, the self-discipline he learnt in captivity now serving him well.  Most of his wounds healed reasonably well, although he dislikes closed spaces, barred windows, dirty conditions and being left alone with strangers.  He can stand any of these, but definitely prefers not to have to.  To a certain extent he lost his insight.  Consider his trust of his mother, despite his past knowledge of her infidelity.

Now David has set to grimly and written up three lists, “People I will never trust again”, “People I might be able to trust” and “People I can and do trust”.  The last list is very short.  He has made a cast-iron resolution never to talk to his mother, who he now refers to as 'Madam', again unless either something drastic happens or social circumstances decree that he absolutely must.  Then he will be coldly formal and very brief.  He will resemble the shadow of his ruthless grandfather as he ferociously carves away all of Amanda's supports.  He feels that if she dies in poverty he won't care at all.  All the bitterness Amanda sowed in David over her treatment of his elder brother, George, will now be given free reign in determining David's actions toward his mother.

Diana is more than happy never to speak to Amanda again, except under very formal and brief circumstances.  She would prefer to cut all ties with Amanda and leave it at that, and she will very gently attempt to steer David into this course of action and away from being an 'avenging fury'.  Amanda's actions may have begun the recent break between Diana and David, but David's lack of action continued it.  Diana has said what she needed to on that matter, but David would do well not to remind her of the hurt he caused her.

To show her continued good faith toward David, in spite of his treatment of her, Diana will take the short list of people he trusts and will place it in a small silver heart shaped locket, a gift from Stephen, that she wears about her neck.  She will also attempt to find out about the other people on the list.  David's judgement has gone astray before, particularly with respect to his mother, and Diana wants to prevent him misplacing his trust again.
