## Appearance

Lisa is small and pretty with short, curly brown hair that is fine, silky, and plentiful.  She is always well dressed, although conservative, and wears little or no jewellery.  Lisa has a very light and slightly sinuous, cat-like gait.  Her skin is fair, smooth, fine-textured, and prone to burning easily.  Her blue eyes have a direct but benevolent gaze.

![[cc_1926_evening_gowns.2.gif]]
## Interests

Lisa works as the assistant to the New York County Coroner, Office of the Medical Examiner, New York City.  Lisa is a qualified doctor and skilled surgeon who has recently chosen to specialise in forensic medicine.

After the sun goes down, Lisa likes to party wherever the best food, drink and entertainment can be found.  She adores cats and admires their dignity and disdain.

## Personality

Lisa is very self-assured with a magnetic personality and a natural capacity to take control and excel in whatever she organises.  This strong autocratic strain is balanced by a generosity of spirit and a very warm-hearted, idealistic nature.  Lisa believes power is to be wielded for the common good.

She is capable of titanic effort when necessity demands and remains composed in a crisis, but can be a very indolent sun worshipper otherwise.  Even though Lisa ignores conventional rules for keeping fit, she still looks and feels her best.  She avoids strenuous sport and has an unbalanced and irregular diet because of her work commitments, aggravated by a fondness for excellent wine and liqueurs.  She never suffers chills because of extremes in the weather.

Lisa likes flattery but is not deceived by it; she likes to be depended on.  She is not a sound judge of people, as she believes that if you treat others right, they will treat you right.  This leads to disillusionment when others disappoint her.

She is not diffident and is always eager to accept challenges, but she cannot stand criticism.  Lisa radiates vitality, inspiration, and loyalty.  Treachery by others is unforgivable.

Lisa is naturally religious with a sublime faith that good will triumph over evil.  a power on high will ensure that justice will prevail here on Earth.  She has high principles and ideals, with a love of humanity.  Lisa believes that love makes the world go round, and she needs to give and receive love in full measure in order to be at her best.

She is very affectionate, protective, and romantic, but can also be very possessive.  Lisa is not eager to marry young and has turned down several opportunities to settle down.  Lisa is not jealous of the attention given to her partner by other women, as she is confident that she has her partner’s complete attention.  She takes rivalry as a form of flattery.

Lisa likes to give gifts to others, but she does not like accepting favours. She has been financially lucky, but she is not money-conscious.  Lisa is very honest in all monetary dealings.

## References

### Medical Jurisprudence - Forensic Medicine

“Forensis” is Latin and means “of the forum”, the meeting place in Rome where civic affairs and legal matters were considered and discussed by those with public responsibilities.  It covers the application of every branch of medical knowledge to the purposes of the law, i.e. the application of medical science to the solution of legal questions that have a medical aspect.

The questions included in modern medical jurisprudence are divided into five general classes:

- those arising out of sexual relations (impotence, sterility, rape, pregnancy and legitimacy);
- injuries inflicted on the living organism (wounds, poisonings, death by violence);
- those arising out of disqualifying diseases (insanity);
- those arising out of deceptive practices such as feigned diseases;
- those of a miscellaneous nature (age, identity).

Medical evidence in a legal proceeding is generally given by a qualified expert and is of two kinds:

- ocular evidence which embraces facts that may be observed by the physician attending a patient;
- testimony based on questions of counsel embodying hypothetical or assumed statements of facts.

In legal proceedings in the United States involving such questions, each party to the actions commonly hires its own experts and, owing to the elicitation of only partial truths by the use of hypothetical questions, equally competent medical experts frequently appear flatly to contradict each other.  The doubt such proceedings have cast on the value of expert opinion has led to the suggestion of many remedial plans as, for example, the establishment of a class of official experts such as now exists in various countries in Europe.

Plans to remedy the situation, however, usually conflict with fundamental principles of the common law, particularly in the conduct of criminal trials: that the court shall be the sole judge of the law, that the jury shall pass upon the facts and that the defendant shall have the right to present any proper evidence on his own behalf.

### History of Forensic Medicine

Although the principles of medical jurisprudence engaged the attention of the earliest lawmakers, the first law providing for expert medical testimony was enacted in 1532 in Germany during the reign of Holy Roman Emperor Charles V.  This law provided that in every case where death had been brought about by violence the opinion of physicians should be sought.

1807 - the first chair in forensic medicine in the English-speaking world was established at the Edinburgh University and filled by Andrew Duncan Jr.

1865 - Alfred Swaine Taylor, Professor of Medical Jurisprudence at Guy’s Hospital Medical School, wrote “Principles and Practice of Medical Jurisprudence”.

1867 - the Medico-Legal Society of the city and state of New York, the first of its kind, was organised.  At the present time many such societies exist in America and Europe.

1898 - Edward Henry, Inspector General of the Bengal Police wrote on the “Classification and Uses of Fingerprints”.

1899 - Bernard Spilsbury at St Mary’s Hospital, Paddington, London wrote copious notes on every case.  He stressed thoroughness at an autopsy and encouraged the development of forensic physics and chemistry.

1902 - Professor John Glaister, Department of Forensic Medicine, Glasgow University wrote “Medical Jurisprudence and Forensic Toxicology”.

1920 - Bernard Spilsbury moved to St Bartholomew’s (Bart’s), a great London teaching hospital, where he lectured on morbid anatomy and histology.  He was knighted in 1921.

A contemporary of Bernard’s was Sidney Alfred Smith, quieter than Spilsbury and an excellent surgeon and forensic pathologist.  Smith contributed to toxicology, microscopy and ballistics.  He was born in New Zealand in 1883 and studied chemistry.  He qualified in medicine at Edinburgh University.  Smith determined that forensics required observation, deductions and a wide range of exact knowledge.

In the early 1920s Smith was appointed medico-legal adviser to the Egyptian Government.  He later founded and became the first professor at the Forensic Department at the University of Cairo.  Poisoning and shooting were rife in Egypt so he became an expert in toxicology and ballistics.

Also in the early 1920s in Egypt, Russel Pasha was the Commandant of the Cairo City Police.

During the 1920s Robert Churchill, a sporting gun maker, owned a shop near Leicester Square which was patronised by rich and titled clients.

1923 - the Bureau of Forensic Ballistics was established in New York.  Charles E. Waite was Head until 1926, when Colonel Calvin Goddard took over.

1926 - the Coroner’s Amendment Act required people to have medical or legal qualifications before they could become a coroner (usually both), with the exception of the USA.  Here the appointment of a coroner is political, no qualifications are needed and the system is open to corruption.  During the 1920s New York abolished the Coroner and paid medical examiners for forensic work.

1928 - Sidney Alfred Smith was appointed Regius Professor of Forensic Medicine at Edinburgh University and Dean of the Medical Faculty, where he wrote his “Textbook of Forensic Medicine”.

Professor Francis Camps (1905 - 1972) was a member of the British Academy of Forensic Sciences and editor of the Academy’s “Medicine, Science and Law” journal.

Dr Milton Helpern (1902 - 1977) worked for 50 years in the Office of the Medical Examiner in New York City.  He was Chief of the office for 25 years.
