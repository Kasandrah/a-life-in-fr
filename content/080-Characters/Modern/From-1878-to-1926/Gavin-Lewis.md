## Appearance

![[cc_gavin_lewis.jpg]]

**Gavin Lewis**

Gavin is tall and slender with sandy brown hair and hazel green eyes.  His hair is short and a lock of it always falls down across his forehead, no matter how he combs it.  He has long legs and arms and a tanned complexion due to spending a lot of time out of doors.  Gavin looks best in tweeds and uniforms.

Gavin has very good eyesight.

## Interests

Gavin is a sportsman who has won school colours at Fives, captained his school house rowing team and played rugger.

When he was 13, Gavin demonstrated accuracy, patience and precision when he began to build model aeroplanes.  He gained an appreciation of fine workmanship in the following years by working on successively more complex and intricate models.

Gavin reads biographies, adventure stories, travel books and sporting literature.  Modern and classical music (Wagnerian) appeals to him.  He smokes a pipe, usually has a dog at his heels and, if the situation allows, a rifle under his arm.

## Personality

Gavin is a go-getter who will let nothing stand in his way – he tackles obstructions head-on.  He is a “man’s man” who spends a lot of time with his male friends, is at his best in male company and popular with his own sex.  He can be quarrelsome, overbearing and very self-centred.

A careerist by temperament, Gavin is especially suited to hazardous and physically exacting occupations.  He did particularly well in the Royal Air Force.  He excels in engineering, and pyrotechnics has a special appeal for him.  He would be completely happy if he took up farming or horse breeding and training.  He would also be very good at the head of a company or as a business promoter.

Gavin is generous to the point of being extravagant, and he believes money should circulate freely.  He is happiest when self-employed.  He is a tough but fair boss who drives himself hard and expects the same of his staff.

## Background

Currently living at 21 Heyington Place, Toorak in Melbourne, Australia.

Gavin’s parents separated while he was at school.  He is their only child.

## Timeline

### 1915

Gavin, now 17 years old, applies to enter the Royal Flying Corps (RFC).  He is interviewed by Lord Hugh Cecil, Staff Captain at the War Office, Whitehall in April 1915 and admitted to the RFC.

On 15th October 1915 Gavin reported to Brooklands for six weeks of training.  He learned to fly and obtained his Pilot’s Certificate in a Maurice Farman “Longhorn” biplane.  He soloed after 1.5 hrs dual training.  He also spent 2 hrs in a “Shorthorn”.

On 28th December 1915 Gavin was commissioned to 2nd Lieutenant via a letter from King George.  Gavin was posted to Gosport for final training on Avros and BE2cs, after which he received his wings.

### 1916

On 19th March 1916 Gavin was posted to No. 22 Squadron as a spare pilot.  He sailed to France to join the squadron at Rouen with 13 flying hours to his credit and a life expectancy of three weeks.

In late March 1916 Gavin was posted to No. 19 Squadron (BE2cs) at Amiens with 20 hours of practice to date.  Here he gained another ten hours practice in a BE2c.  His first duty was to photograph enemy trenches.  After being shot down by a Fokker and completing an emergency landing, Gavin was assigned to ferry planes across the English Channel.

In May 1916 Gavin spent his 18th birthday in Rouen, France.  He received notice to proceed to No. 1 Aircraft Depot, St. Omer under General Officer Commanding RFC “Boom” Trenchard.  Here Gavin practiced on a BE2c and flew a Bristol Bullet, but only once.  The Bristol Bullet was a tiny machine too small for Gavin’s long legs; he fouled the dashboard with his knees and could hardly use the rudder.  Too light on the controls, Gavin crashed (flipped over) on landing on a gusty day.

Gavin also trained in a Parasole Morane, a two-seater monoplane noted as being a death trap for inexperienced pilots.  It required great skill and care, light hands and accurate judgement to land.  He flew a BE2c against the first captured Fokker in tests at St. Omer and ferried a Parasole to Gosport (England) and an FE from Farnborough back to St. Omer (2hs each way over the Channel).

In late May 1961, Gavin was posted to No. 3 Squadron, the Somme, La Houssaye.  A squadron usually consisted of twelve planes; three flights of four machines each.  No. 3 Squadron consisted of two flights of Parasole monoplanes and one flight of biplanes.  Gavin undertook artillery observation, photography and contact patrols with British ground forces.

In July 1916, Gavin was shot down over the trenches, made and emergency landing undamaged and walked back to safety through the wasteland of the 1st July offensive.  He watched gas attacks from above and was totally horrified and revolted.  He took a ground trip to the front to see the front line batteries and to experience the conditions of trench warfare.

In August 1916, Gavin attacked two Fokkers; one fought back almost shooting down the Parasole and demonstrating its ability to shot between the rotations of its own propeller, a feat achieved by the Germans before the Allies.  Gavin ferried a BE12, a single seater BE2c configured as a recon plane, back from Farnborough to Candas after two weeks leave in England, after which he returned to St. Omer.

On 3rd September 1916, while flying low and machine-gunning trenches, a bullet came up through the floor of the cockpit missing Gavin’s ankle by half an inch.  Gavin’s plane averaged 20 to 30 bullet holes on each patrol.

On 15th September 1916 at 6.30 am, Gavin undertook his first contact patrol with the new British tanks.  The following day Gavin completed another successful emergency landing at an advance airfield after taking a bullet through the oil tank and propeller.

The last week in September and the first week in October saw Gavin at home on sick leave suffering from conjunctivitis, a consequence of flying without goggles for observation work.  In late October 1916 Gavin, flying a new plane, suffered from conjunctivitis again.

Also in late October, Gavin was awarded the Military Cross and after eight months overseas, including four months of the Somme battle and 350 hours flying time, Gavin was posted back to England for Home Establishment duty.

### 1916 – 1917

From winter 1916 to the end of March 1917, Gavin was posted to the Testing Squadron at Upavon where he tested all of the new aircraft before orders were placed with manufacturers.  This he did before parachutes existed.  While testing at Martlesham Heath Gavin flew the Sopwith Triplane scout, which he remembers as providing the most flying pleasure of all the planes he flew during the war.

In March 1917, just before his 18th birthday, Gavin was posted to London Colney to join No. 56 Squadron, Major Bloomfield, C.O., forming to go overseas.  His plane was a SE5 fighting scout from the Royal Aircraft Factory, a plane with a 140 hp Hispano Suiza engine, one synchronised Vickers gun firing through the propeller via Constantinesco gears, one Lewis gun on top – a first class fighting scout.

On 7th April 1917, starting at 11.00 am, No. 56 Squadron flew to France.  Captain Ball DSO was the Senior Flight Commander.  As the squadron leader had been injured in a car accident on the previous evening, Gavin led the squadron overseas.  No. 56 set the record for the first squadron to arrive in France with all planes fully functional.  The squadron was located in Vertgaland and two weeks later went on its first offensive patrol.

On 7th May 1917 eleven planes from No. 56 Squadron went out gunning for Richthofen and his circus of red Albatrosses.  They attacked six Hun scouts and were in turn attacked by more red Albatrosses guarding the first six.  Only five of the original eleven planes from No. 56 Squadron returned and Captain Ball DSO was lost.

In June 1917, Gavin tested a new 200 hp engine in an SE5.  He attained 22,000 feet over England and glided all the way back to France.  He returned to England for one week with No. 56 Squadron as a defensive measure against German Gothas bombing London.  Here Gavin was stationed at Bekesbourne.

Gavin took an SE to Dunkirk to show it to a wing of French pilots so that they would know what it looked like and not shoot it down.  The French wing included the “Storks” Spads squadron led by the French ace Guynemeyer (shot down and killed one week later).

Gavin was hit in the shoulder when attacked by a Pfaltz scout from out of the sun in the early morning, suffering a six-inch glancing wound.  This gained him two weeks leave at home and a wound stripe.

On return from leave Gavin was promoted to Flight Commander (Captain) and posted to No. 44 Squadron at Hainault Farm, Ilford in Essex.  This squadron consisted of Sopwith Camels with 110 hp Clerget engines and two Vickers guns each and served as Home Defense.

In August 1917 Gavin undertook his first night patrols against the German bombers.

In September 1917 Gavin was posted to No. 61 Squadron, Rochford and continued his Home Defence role.  He used a Sopwith Pup for joyrides and an SE5 for fighting.  While flying in fog Gavin became completely lost – landing in Kent when he thought he was in Essex.

In November 1917, after going up at night with magnesium wing flares ignited and spinning his plane “because it would look awfully pretty”, Gavin botched his cross wind approach, broke the undercarriage on landing and flipped the plane.  After this crash, Gavin had to use a new Hispano with no instrument lighting for night patrols.  On one such flight his torch broke and he couldn’t read the oil pressure gauge.

Also in November 1917, Gavin was given flying badges from a captured German pilot he was escorting to POW camp.  He flew an SE from Brooklands Aircraft Acceptance Park to St. Omer in pouring rain at a height of 100 feet.  He returned to Calais by tender and took the leave boat back to London

### 1917 – 1918

The RFC merged with the RNAS to become the Royal Air Force (RAF) and Gavin wore a new blue uniform.

Gavin performed gas mask drill in a room full of chlorine gas.  On one flight his Pup caught fire in the air, and then the fire went out.  He advised the Sopwith Works staff on new planes, conducted wireless telephony tests on scout aircraft, met Lily Elsie and Ivor Novello and made friends with Bertram Jones at the Air Ministry.  He landed a plane to ask for directions but, when starting it up again on his own, the plane took off too quickly and raced around in circles until it crashed into a hedge.

On 2nd October 1918, as Senior Flight Commander of No. 152 Night Flying Squadron, Gavin flew back to France in one of the squadron’s special Camels built with the pilot and tanks reversed.  The squadron’s destination was Faukenbury and Gavin flew over with C.O. Henty’s bull terrier puppy in the back of the plane.  The squadron suffered no mishaps or lost planes.

In early November 1918 Gavin’s flight was detached to Houthulst via Ypres, where he saw the total devastation caused to the whole area by the war.  On 11th November 1918, Armistice Day, Gavin rejoined No. 152 Night Flying Squadron at Carvin.

Three weeks later, at 20 years of age, he joined Vickers Aviation Department under General Caddell.  Here he worked on converting Vickers “Vimys” with two Rolls Royce engines into passenger planes that were then sold to start up airlines.  He drew up schedules of expenditure, running expenses and possible profits to be derived from starting air services in different parts of the world.

### 1919

In early 1919, Gavin flew a Vimy from Upavon to Brooklands for later conversion and use in the first Atlantic crossing (15 hours and 57 minutes, John Alcock pilot and Whitten Brown navigator).  Gavin’s name is in the front page of the logbook.

In June and July 1919, Gavin was part of a Flying Circus with Tommy Broome, Stanley Cockerell, some air mechanics, some Avros bought from the Aircraft Disposal Board and modified to take two passengers, and some good humour.  He flew at Cardiff, Birmingham, Sheffield and Newcastle giving exhibitions, advertising and running special charters.  Gavin finished his barnstorming career in September 1919.

In October 1919, Gavin arranged to go to China where Vickers Limited was contracted to supply 60 training machines (Avros) and 40 commercial Vimys, spares, engines, etc. to form a training school for Chinese pilots for the Peking to Shanghai run.  Gavin was one of two pilots, supported by eight crew.

### 1920

In April 1920 Gavin arrived in Peking, but no planes arrived until December 1920.  Through June and July 1920 Gavin suffered rheumatic fever in his left ankle and wrist and was confined to bed for two months.  In August 1920 Gavin went to Tsingtao where the sun and seawater healed him.  In September 1920 Gavin returned to Peking and in October 1920 he bought a Chinese house, furnishing it appropriately.

On 17th December 1920 the planes finally arrived in Peking.  Gavin and the other pilot started training the Chinese students.

### 1921

In February 1921 an unsuccessful first attempt was made to fly between Peking and Shanghai.  The Peking – Shanghai Air Service had been ordered to run on the first day of the monsoons.  In May 1921 Gavin returned to London, where he continued working for Vickers Limited.

### 1922

In January 1922 Gavin was sent by Vickers Limited to Melbourne, Australia to support the Melbourne sales agency.  He tested and demonstrated Vickers aircraft shipped to Melbourne from London.  He sold them to starting air mail carriers, private pilots and joyflighters.
