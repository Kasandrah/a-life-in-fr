# Rhodri's Tales - 495 AD

**Written by _[[Ben-G|Ben G.]]_ (Rhodri)**

Before our Lord King and Master, Arthur, raised these lands from an abandoned outpost of the old Empire to its noble heights as the centre of The New Empire, there were men of deeds and purpose who were there to blazon the way.  Men such as whose names would be renowned across the lands for their achievements and their mysteries.

The most famed band of all in those times was that led by _[[010-Knights-of-Arthurian-Britain#Lord Rhodri|Lord Rhodri]]_, our Earl of Jagent.  Others of this celebrated company were _[[010-Knights-of-Arthurian-Britain#Sir Arcavius|Sir Arcavius]]_, our Praetor of Dorset, and the _[[010-Knights-of-Arthurian-Britain#Lord Cynon|Earl of Rydychan]]_.  With the passing of time, these deeds and names have become hazed over with the ravages of age and newer wonders.

The High King has demanded that this history be not forgotten, and has ordered my humble self to write this history as told to me by the Right Honourable, Earl Rhodri.  He begins these chronicles in the year of Rome 1248, or as in the calendar of the Christians, 495 Anno Domini.

As told by Lord Rhodri

Before I would talk on myself, I would tell you somewhat of my father.  He was just another mercenary knight, as were so many others of that time.  Just another, but he had a dream, a desire that kept him fighting.  He was driven to despair as he saw how low his beloved Britannia, luminary of the Empire, had stooped.  All the glory that was Britannia, hardened within the forge of the Empire, was fading, ... fading away.  He searched for a lord who seemed different.  A lord who looked different from the others; someone who remembered what it was to be part of the Empire, to be part of a noble culture which showed the beauty within humanity.  Surely that was worth fighting for.

And fight he did.  Long and hard.  Until there were no more wild, uncouth barbarians to fight. .... Or until the shining light of Imperial Rome was stilled within the eyes while the spilt blood cooled and the spirit stole away.  Although the latter was more tragic, my father feared the former.  For each lord who was victorious, anger and betrayal crept in as the notions of Rome and the ideals they had espoused were forgotten or spurned.  In little time, with no pressure of conflict to cloud the vision, the fact that the barbarians had been defeated lay more and more open to question.

I followed my father till I was 18 years of age, and then when he went to take service with Uther Pendragon against the rebellious Duke Gorlois of Cornwall, he travelled to Sarum to seek a place for myself within the squires of Salisbury to give me more prestige and training.  He had trained me well in the arts of war, but I needed more than those skills to continue my father's work and help return Britannia to her former glory.  I had hoped my father would be proud of me when he returned from those fights, but he did not return.  He died in battle down in Cornwall, finally succumbing to the barbarism he had fought all his life.  That was in the year 1245.  Of the next 3 years, there is little to tell.

## The Squires

Within the keeping of _[[030-Arthurian-Britain#^48ddcb|Earl Robert of Salisbury]]_ were a number of squires, but of these some were more advanced and under the especial charge of _[[030-Arthurian-Britain#^9de48d|Sir Jaradan]]_, the Castellan of Vagon Castle and the Marshal of Salisbury.  He was an old veteran who had a penchant for telling his war stories to anyone who listened, but he was the Earl's right-hand man for council.  I found though that when we talked of his old battles, I knew more of the tactics than he did.  But do not doubt that he was a terrible tyrant to those who broke the rules.

But still, amongst us squires were few who were exceptional.  There was a short Irish lad who was a few years younger than I, but had a fierceness to him that could be terrible to behold.  His name was _[[010-Knights-of-Arthurian-Britain#Sir Brion|Brion]]_.  He was incisive and vicious with the sword.  Quick to take offence, and quick to fight, even against the greatest of odds.  He was practically everything I wasn't, but still I found a great friendship growing with him.  One more of the squires became a friend, and that was _[[030-Arthurian-Britain#^0711d0|Edar of Huntingdon]]_.  There didn't appear to be much to him, but he was always there.  There was also one who had been a squire for many years, named _[[030-Arthurian-Britain#^e7a766|Hywel]]_ but he was distant from all, in a brooding way, even from the ladies and serving-girls who seemed to flock to him.  Then, of course, there was I, knowledgeable of battle, skilled in melee, light of finger across the harp, and nimble a-horse.  And, nay, I was not modest.

## The Man-Eating Bear

But, as I told thee, I begin my tale in the year 1248.  The Marshal of this fair Earldom, the none-so-fair Sir Jaradan, gathered us squires together for some peasants had reported a man-eating bear in the west acres.  They claimed it was too dangerous for them to come and work the lord's fields.  As he had never heard of such a thing, "Man-eating bear, indeed!" he was sending some of us squires to this village.  Obviously due to my bearing and abilities, the Marshal assigned me as leader and asked which two squires would I lief take with me.  Of course, I chose Brion and Edar.  Who else would I take on an adventure!  Amidst the excitement, I barely heard Sir Jaradan instruct me to go to the village of Imber and ask for Old Garr, the priest, who would direct us further.

From Sarum, it was but a short journey of half a day to the village.  But I fear that the excitement of being on our own delayed us somewhat.  We passed Amesbury Abbey and the standing stones of Stonehenge, as well as a few manors, along the way, trying boyishly to play the man.  But night was approaching as we entered Imber at the boom of the valley of the down.  The old priest who had awaited us welcomed us and we found little choice but to stay at his hovel for the night.  It was not until the next morn that we found that the village was large but poor, with almost all the villagers seeming to exist by hunting.  As Old Garr led us through his village, we saw all the menfolk must be out hunting.  In fact, Old Garr himself was an old hunter, and he told us of the missing hunters and that bear tracks were found in the forest.

Expecting little, but eager for a fight, we rode through the forest trying to find the trail of one animal - the bear.  A trail that we found, and followed.  Brion saw it first, and lowered his spear and charged at it, with a lilting, Gaelic curse.  I spurred my horse to charge as well, but I was well led by my slighter companion.  I am sure that Edar, as he invariably did, was bringing up the rear.  I chose to throw my spear aside and draw my sword.  It looked a poor choice to use a weapon with which you had little ability with, when you were superior to most others with another weapon.  Unfortunately for Brion, his aim with the swaying spear was off and then I was upon the animal.  I smote the beast soundly enough to make it sway.  It attacked us as we passed but it only managed to strike Brion, dealing him a savage blow.  We turned our horses and charged the beast again.  This time it fell and did not rise.  Bursting with pride in our selves, we whooped and thought to take this bear's fur back with us.  But before we even assayed it, we thought that our amateur efforts would ruin such a fur.  And why should we … when we have grateful hunters nearby who could do it much better than we ever could.

## Sir Marchlew

On our return to Imber, we rode in as victors of battle, favoured of the god Mithras.  And the cheers of the grateful villagers were very satisfying.  It was with a feeling of magnanimity that we gifted the villagers with the bear's carcass for a feast tonight and claimed the fur of the man-eater.

As the preparations for the feast were begun, our elation began to fade as we began to really notice the lack of men folk in Imber.  A number of elderly hunters went out to get the bear, but that left only the children and the women.  As the rest of the village celebrated, we questioned Old Garr as he bandaged Brion, and he only told us that the village was under the demesne of Sir Marchlew.  It was only when we pressed him hard on this that he told us quietly that their lord had for some while been carting off adults and children away …somewhere.

This was hard to believe, for Sir Marchlew was one of Earl Robert's foremost knights and long-time advisor, and surely such bad stewardship of your own holdings would have been below him, but the proof was hard for us to ignore as it was all around us.  Suddenly finding our high spirits brought low, we now only played the part of the villagers' heroes and stayed another night with the priest.

On our return ride back to Sarum, we argued about what we should do.  For although it was bad stewardship, it was his demesne!  It didn't necessarily mean that something untoward was happening, but still … this was barbaric!  One more sign of the decay that all true knights should oppose, surely. Then again, what should we do … what could we do?

If we went to Earl Robert and accused Sir Marchlew of bad stewardship, what would he really do except instruct Sir Jaradan on the proper behaviour of squires, which would in turn mean physical instruction on our lack of respect for our superiors.  In the end, all we could really think of was to tell Sir Jaradan and see what he would do.

And that is what we did … after he had soundly praised us for the good job we did in Imber.  Just as it had left such a bitter taste in our mouths and left us bereft of what to do now, so was Sir Jaradan struck dumb.

## The Signal Tower

Umming and ahhing, he told us that while he believed us, he had another task for us to do while he decided how to handle this.  One of the south-western signal-towers which was west of Winton, along the Lower Wylye River, had not been heard from in a few days.  We were to ride out there and investigate.  He expected it was just a case of running out of wood or maybe some men-at-arms need some extra discipline, like some squires he could mention.  Even though we felt we had proven ourselves to be men, I fear that his hard gaze and stern tone were still enough to make us instinctively flee to our next task.

Now don't go and think that these towers are the stone keeps they are now, these signal-towers were just a bare wooden tower frame with a wooden palisade around its base.  But they still used them the same way, the guards could light fires in braziers set atop the tower that would be seen some way off by the next tower.  This line of towers stretched around most of Salisbury, and was Earl Robert's first line of warning of any incursions into his demesnes.  If there were any problems with one of them, then any warning might not reach Earl Robert until too late.  This particular tower was about a day's ride from Sarum.  So we decided to set out straight away so we could spend the night at Winton, and arrive at the tower by midday.

At Winton, the _[[030-Arthurian-Britain#^a56347|Lady Gaille]]_ guested us right well but I found myself eager to go on early as she was quite keen for us all to stay much longer.  Brion was also quite keen to go first thing in the morning, but I believe it was because he was just too energetic to sit still long enough to break his fast.  So it was mid-morning when we approached the silent tower.  And silent was the word that should not have been; Brion and myself felt that something was awry before we came close.  We stopped to look and quietly talk to each other.  As we were talking, we spotted some movement within the tower and were quite surprised to see that it was a Saxon!

Hoping that we hadn't been seen, we dismounted and tied the horses to one of the nearby trees and crept forward with our weapons drawn.  But as we drew near, it was all I could do to hold back my cry of surprise for Sir Marchlew was there as well talking with some of these Saxons.  Now that we had moved close, we could see some signs of battle at the gate of the palisade, such that it couldn't be closed anymore.  Flashing a quick smile at Brion, this seemed a gift straight from Mithras.  Sir Marchlew was plainly working with the Saxons, thus solving any other need for us to find some other proof.  And there didn't seem to be that many Saxons, so we could solve the problem right here and right now.  With but a smile and a nod, we began to move forward.

We were almost through the gate before they noticed us, and by that time it was too late to stop us from getting through them.  Edar and I found us a Saxon apiece, while Brion managed to race past aiming directly for Sir Marchlew.  I slew my Saxon opponent and found that I had two new opponents, but I was able to throw a quick glance at Brion and Marchlew who were fighting there way up the tower structure.  With some of the swings that my father had taught me, I more than managed to fend them off. I slew them both, and looked up in time to see Marchlew fall to the ground nearby.  Brion yelled wordlessly above, and clambered down much quicker than he had gone up.  Marchlew was not slain, he had been barely able to hold Brion off and had continued backing away, even beyond the edge of the tower.

Elated at having fought so well, it took Edar's common sense to remind us that we had to get back to Winton to arrange for new sentries for this tower.  For even though these Saxons were dead, there might more coming.  Then Edar gave Marchlew some quick aid so he could be returned to the custody of Earl Robert.  As we walked back to our horses, we discussed that probably Marchlew was selling his people as slaves to the Saxons and had stepped over the boundary from working with the Saxons to working for the Saxons.  I was sure that the earl's men would drag the truth from the traitor and that he would suffer the just spoils of treachery.

## Merffyn and the Boy King

Thinking to return as heroes, we were surprised to find everyone rushing around.  It took us two days before Sir Jaradan would see us.  In that time, we found out that there was news from the south.  The Saxons had landed into the forests east of Dorchester, and they had possibly brought enough of themselves to stay.  At the moment they were moving either to Dorchester or towards Sarum!  Sir Jaradan heard what we had to tell him, and I think he was pleased with us … because he didn't say anything at all about it except "good."  Relaxing his normal scowl, which struck us dumb, he rewarded us with some relaxing message relays to _[[030-Arthurian-Britain#^1cd1b1|Praetor Jonathel of Dorset]]_.  I seem to recall him gruffly explaining it so we wouldn't have to suffer the boredom of training while the army gathered.

We were sent south along the road joining Sarum with Dorchester, with the mixed feelings of hoping that the Saxon army hadn't reached the road yet, but that some individual Saxons had, so we could prove ourselves again.  However, the only person that we met on the way was an old, yet spry, man who came on us when we had to camp on our return journey.  We knew there was something out of the ordinary, for he had a silver-furred wolf as a companion.  But it was a bolt from the heavens to find that our new campfire companion was the legendary _[[Merlin|Merffyn]]_: he who had raised Stonehenge, amongst many other renowned feats.  He must have sensed something in me for, with a twinkle in his eye, he offered me a view of the future.  Looking at my companions, I recall that Edar was shaking his head at me while Brion just stared stony-eyed at Merffyn.  I felt strangely bemused when he took me to a tree and said that we would travel through it.  For a moment, I thought that I was only with a crazy old man, but then I was sucked somehow into the tree.

Although I was to learn later that we travelled along a Ley Line, at that time, I only knew that we moved very fast through solid rock and I didn't like the feeling it gave me.  It made me feel ill to my stomach, but when we arrived at our destination, Merffyn showed me my future … the future of Britain and the Britons.  Peering out of the cave we had fallen into, I looked out onto the beach and saw a small tot.  At first, I didn't realize who it was but a feeling of recognition grew in me.  As Merffyn began to tell me who it was, I realized I already knew.  The boy was named Arthur, and he was the only heir to Uther Pendragon, he who had been the High King of the Britons.  I wanted to rush out and pay homage, but Merffyn said that we had to leave.  So I could only promise to serve and protect him for as long as I lived for his and for the Empire's sake.  Rome may be falling to darkness, but we can rebuild it anew here.  But I digress, Merffyn returned me back to my companions and I returned to Sarum in a state of wonder and hope.

## The Battle of Wareham Woods

As the Earl completed gathering his own forces, including a number of mercenaries who had served until earlier that year with Uther Pendragon in Cornwall, we were given the signal honours of being made squires to Earl Robert himself … his 7th, 8th and 9th ones I seem to recall.  Even if we had failed to understand the mark of distinction we had been given, then Sir Jaradan was able to remind us quite strenuously about it.  Within the army, a squire's normal role is to take care of his lord.  On the battlefield, it is to take in hand any captives their lord might take, also to provide any weapons or vittles that their lord might require, and to provide first-aid or seek to recover their lord if he should fall.  Sir Jaradan had also been at some pains to ensure we remembered what we were to do.  See! I still remember.

Anyway, the Saxon forces still had not split our forces, so the forces of Earl Robert and Praetor Jonathel were able to quite easily unite and move south-eastwards against the advancing Saxons.  In the battle that came to be called after the nearby Wareham Woods, it fell to the Earl of Salisbury to command our forces as the most senior noble.  Local foresters were able to tell him of a fairly cleared area which the Saxons hadn't reached.  And when we arrived, we could see the lay of the land was favourable for a defender if the attacker came from the direction of where the Saxons were coming from.  So Earl Robert set our order of battle, and arrayed our forces appropriately and sent out the scouts to locate the enemy, and followed with skirmishers to entice them to this battle site.

Come the Saxons did … in larger numbers than we had heard of.  The horde before us was at least 3 times our size and there were more still in the woodlands behind them.  When I fought behind my father years before, I had thought I had experienced battle.  But this was my first real battle.  The spectacle before me in just the manoeuvrings before it showed that I had just been in skirmishes.  The lines were drawn no more than 100 feet apart, and the taunts were thrown at each other in languages the other couldn't understand.  I can't really tell you how much noise there is with 2 large armies facing each other.  I know it is loud, but not as loud as when one or both of the opposing armies decides it is time and begins its charge.  Then … you can only hear your own yell and know that you could die.  Mithras, that was so many years ago and I can still hark back to that day.

As is the way of battle, the plans you may have before the battle seem to devolve into separate individual whorls of combat even before your eyes.  I saw Earl Robert with a large knot of men striving to reach the standards of the Saxons, even as they were doing the same.  But as the hours progressed, us squires could see that although our forces were slaying large numbers of the Saxons, but for every five of them that were slain one of our own knights were lost.  This still left the odds against us the same.  The god of battle, Mithras, gives opportunities to those who see enough to take them.  The vagaries of the battle had produced a lessening in the numbers of Saxons in a curving line between us and the slowly approaching Saxon leaders, while the nearest units of knights, including that of the Earl's, were being swamped by Saxon numbers.  Turning to my fellow squires, I could see that Brion had been champing at the bit to be involved in the battle anyway, and when I started throwing orders at some of the others, we formed our own band to take this opportunity from Mithras.

Charging our light horses into the battle, we rode over the few enough Saxons in this breach of the Saxons striking some as we stormed past.  And all too soon we found ourselves facing the largest Saxons and obvious Saxon chieftains we had ever seen.  Mind you, looking back from now, I have seen larger ones since, but I'm telling you of the novice in his first real battle.  At the forefront of our squire charge were Brion, Edar and myself.  Thinking back, I know that the others were there, but I think only we front 3 actually fought against the chieftains.  Fought and won!  Although, Edar was slow enough with his that Brion and I managed to finish his off for him.

We took the Saxon standards, and I felt we were likely to hold them for but a short time, but the Saxons morale was broken while our forces were uplifted.  All across the battlefield, the tide of battle had reached its peak and began to ebb away.  Only a dribble at first, but then it slowly became a flood of fleeing Saxons.  It was surprising to find that the whole morning and most of the afternoon had already fled, so there were few who had the energy to chase down the Saxons.  This was probably not too bad a thing, for I still thought the Saxons outnumbered our remaining army.

## Knighted in Battle

As the Earl, the Praetor and the growing number of knights behind them approached us, I felt proud of us all for doing such a courageous act in this battle.  But as I caught the eye of Sir Jaradan, I had a sudden flash of guilt, as I thought of how many things we did which a squire just doesn't do, but that was pushed down by the knowledge that what I had done was right for Britannia.  So it was that the knights came to us squires, who bore the captured Saxon flags.  And there, before all of the knights of Salisbury and Dorset, Earl Robert praised us for showing the true virtues of knighthood and with a nod from the Praetor knighted us 3 there amongst the bodies of our enemies.  No brother, I just have some dust in my eye. I think that's enough for now, anyway.

Yes, brother!  I've told you already, I'm quite comfortable here.  Now, where were we up to?  Oh yes, the new knights of Salisbury.  It was a bright and happy time for a while.  Everybody was treating us like heroes.  Now surely I don't have to tell you, brother, how it feels to be raised from treated almost like nobodies to being noted by all who had treated you like … well, a retainer, I guess.  Hmm, maybe I do …. I'm sorry; I didn't mean to insult you.  Well, what I'm trying to say is that we were somewhat feted, and became eligible to some eyes.  The ladies of the court were viewed us much more favourably than before.  They actually saw us now.

But I am afraid that back then, I was not favourably disposed in the way of form.  No, it is true, brother.  The long scar on my face you see now was much rawer and I was predisposed to wearing the long moustaches of my forefathers to cover it somewhat.  Even to myself, I could not hide my appearance.  But, my companions were better looking than most, with Edar being quite the ladies' knight.  But before we could grow comfortable with such graces, we were shown the duties that we must give our liege lord.

## Sir Gondarins

Our first task given to us was to be the escort for our Earl Robert's eldest son and heir, _[[030-Arthurian-Britain#^395376|Sir Gondarins]]_.  Mostly, this was a simple task of accompanying him about the court.  Fortunately for us, we thought then, Sir Gondarins was granted the task of patrolling the western and south-western borders of Salisbury.  Afterwards as we prepared for the journey, Sir Jaradan was able to pass individual instructions to us.  If you couldn't already guess, we were instructed to keep Sir Gondarins out of trouble.  I used to think that things would change when I was knighted, but it hadn't been too long before I was disabused of that misconception.

We travelled westward from Sarum along the main road to Vagon Castle.  Even though this leg of the journey was a day's easy ride, we managed to restrain ourselves from stopping at the walled city of Wilton and the certain hospitality of the garrulous Lady Gaille, and continued on past the stark, but beautiful thorn-and-wild flower covered Grovely Castle … yes, that's right brother, that is the name of that ancient fort atop the great earthen-works.  Although we stayed at Vagon Castle that late afternoon and night, we were eager to move on.  There were things out-of-kilter there, but we couldn't quite decide what it was and with our lack of experience, we felt it was not our place to question the castellan or his lord.

From Vagon, our plan was to travel southwards through the forest edges to the western end of the Ebble River valley.  We would then pass through the lands of the _[[030-Arthurian-Britain#^fef3ea|Lady Jeanne of Broadchalke]]_ along the Ebble, all the way to Ebble Castle, and then return northwards along that main road back to Sarum.  I recall it was in fact a very pleasant ride.  Beautiful views, amusing company, young spirits and no trouble made it notable, not as much as what would follow, but still memorable in and of itself.  We crossed rolling wooded hills and an old ford, before we had to head deeper into the forest to cross over into the river valley.  I knew that there were many dangers in the forest, but it didn't seem to matter then.

## Karwin and Anwyn

![[da_karwin.jpg]]

**Karwin**

But what we did chance upon was a strange and wondrous thing, indeed, brother!  For in the shade of some trees by a stream, we rested briefly to break our fast in the day.  And there rolled into our midst a strangely made ball from the woods.  Closely followed by sound of hooves.  So closely that we had no time to react, which was probably a good thing - for we would surely have startled our guest.  A small colt, with a horn grown from above its eyes, hurtled out of the brush and jumped onto the ball.  Of course, it couldn't … what was that, brother?  Yes, yes, yes, of course it was a unicorn!  Do you think there are other creatures of a similar description?  If you don't mind, please read out what I've said last … Ah yes, of course, it couldn't stay on the ball, for it's leap had much energy behind it, and he tumbled past us, leaving us looking askance at ourselves, and then at the rolling ball.

Following somewhat behind came a man who we did react to.  He had been calling out the name "Karwin" in the tone of voice of an exasperated parent.  When he did see us, he was a little startled, but he very quickly regained his poise.  While we started to talk, "Karwin" came back to our visitor, Anwyn, and nuzzled him.  Now Anwyn was a tall man of striking looks, none more so than the diamond-shaped mark in the centre of his forehead.  Our wonder at this unicorn was only less than the growing wonder as we each put it together that we were talking with a male unicorn in human form.  Being charmed by the blithe spirit of _[[Karwin|Karwin]]_ and the grace of his father, I know now, _[[Lord-Anwyn|Anwyn]]_, the afternoon passed swiftly until we had to continue our patrol.

## The Raven Priestess

It was coming on nightfall when we approached Chalke Castle, the demesne of Lady Jeanne.  Now let me point out to you, brother, that I had met her before at the court, for she was the chief handmaiden of the _[[030-Arthurian-Britain#^48ddcb|Countess Katherine]]_, wife of our Earl Robert.  I had seen that she was very beautiful, but I had never really entertained any other thoughts than does a young man looking at a very beautiful woman … are you blushing, brother?  I'm sorry; I fear I will make you blush more.

Standing in the lit gateway, she was a vision of welcome and I felt my heart sway.  After the afternoon joy of meeting the unicorns, this felt right. I had a sudden, sharp pang of yearning for this to be home, for her to look at me with more than favour.  Welcome us in, she did, and gave thanks that we had arrived safely.  After she bade her servants to see to our needs, she hosted us in the main hall and told us of her troubles of late.  Over the past few months, her flocks of sheep had wandered into the surrounding forest and there had been some losses.  It had been thought that it was just some wild beasts.  Just in the past month, those losses had increased, and some of the farmer folk had also had some losses.  It had then been thought that there might be some bandits or robber knights out in the woods.  Just in the past week, two suitors had come and entered the forest to chase these bandits, but they had not returned.  In the midst of a small host of cries that we would help, none louder than I, she quietly told us that she believed that the creatures out there were werewolves.  I swear to you by Mithras, brother, that there was a strange, snarling howl from out in the woods, then.

Brion and I jumped up and swore that not another day should rise before this would end.  Although Sir Gondarins was slow to agree, we convinced him of the merits of our vow.  And so we ventured out into the night, back into the forest below the light of the full moon.  Leaving our horses behind, we entered the forest afoot, the better to try and hunt these creatures.  I tell you now, that it was my friend and squire, Hywel, who showed he was quite adept at tracking, even by the light of the moon, for he did find the tracks of wolves, but larger than he had ever seen.

We were prepared to do battle with monsters, creatures from myth and legends … but we were unprepared for what we did meet first.  This was to be the first of unfortunately many meetings, with another creature from myths and legends.  But let me set the scene for you first … the woods were close around us, and the brush was quite thick around.  To reach this small "open" area that only just fit our party, we had to scrape past some thorn bushes.  The night had been quiet, with not even the sounds of insects to distract us.  But then from the far distance, we began to hear the soft sounds of something approaching, originally from our left flank, but then seemingly before us, and then jumping about before seeming to be all around us.  I swore that I heard the sounds of horses tack but horses should not be able to pass through such almost impenetrable undergrowth as were passing through.  But pass through these horses did … into quite a large clearing where we were.  Yes, brother, I know I said that it was barely a clearing, but the change in size arises from the change in what we saw, not from a change in my memory.  Stop looking at me like that, brother, please …

## Oberon, King of the Fae

Ah, yes, before us now in the large clearing was plainly a hunting party.  The party of nobles' ahorse, armed with spears, and trailed by a band of followers on foot, armed with bows, and with a pack of dogs coming to heel by one of the men.  However, it was very plain to all of us that we were not meeting anyone human.  All had such a cast to their face that bespoke many things, but nothing human.  They all had pointed ears, where the ears could be seen.  Now the nobles were dressed in whites, and silvers and golds, which all seemed to shine.  The others were in varying shades of green.  The horses looking much grander than any horse I had ever seen before, but the hounds were only vaguely recognisable as hounds.  They had all the same features, but they, too, were of a different, and more vicious, cast.  I knew from the stories that this could not be Herne and his Wild Hunt, and for that I was thankful, but I feared that this was a party of Fae.

I am sorry but I cannot recall the actual words spoken between us, but I tell you that we were facing _[[Oberon|King Oberon]]_, of the Seelie Court of Fae, himself.  One of the Fae afoot spoke with us, while all the rest looked at us impassively and totally still.  On our behalf, Brion and I spoke carefully and with great trouble to not provoke Oberon in any way, without belittling our own honour.  I think we did rather well, to be able to hold a conversation at all with them.  It came as a great surprise, more to Oberon than to us, that we were hunting the same quarry.  I recall that Oberon, who had been quite stone-faced, suddenly gurgled with laughter when I told them we sought to slay the werewolves.  When he quietened himself somewhat, he leaned forward over his horse's neck and pointed out a direction and said that we would find them a few minutes that way.  When we offered him the privilege of the conclusion of his own hunt, he declined with a smile.

## Werewolves

We parted, more grateful to leave them, than for their help in our hunt.  And I avow, brother, that the bushes and thorns and such easily let us pass.  No one was scratched, and it was like walking through a field of long wheat.  After meeting Oberon and striving to keep our tongues in leash, what followed was almost a comedown of tension.  As careful as we tried to be after such a strange meeting in the night, we were almost unready when the werewolves ambushed us.  There were four of them, and they were big.  How big, brother?  Bigger than any of us there I know!  Umm, I'd say that they were just shorter than a bear rearing up.  Although some light broke through the trees, it wasn't really enough to show us much of them before they were amongst us.  It was frantic work to make sure none of the squires had to face one of them, but soon enough all four off us knights were facing one of those beasts.

Within minutes, all of us had realised that our best blows were at the very most giving them only cuts and scrapes … that our weapons just weren't doing any real damage.  We struggled to continue striking them, as well as fending off their bites and claws.  But it was Sir Gondarins who was the first to fail and be bitten through his shoulder.  Everyone thought that he was probably infected now, so we redoubled our efforts, and with the assistance of Hywel, we fought to disengage ourselves and get Gondarins to the castle.  But the monsters would not let us go.

Brion was becoming more and more frenzied, but then he slowed briefly and said in his lilting way that he had an idea, so we must hold on.  Diving forward, he rolled past his opponent, bounced up and ran … back the way we had come.  Nay, don't look so shocked, brother, a battle is not just foes standing opposite each other while hitting each other until only one is standing; it is much more than that.  I found out from him afterwards that he had almost set himself to die, but that he then remembered that the Fae were hunting these werewolves and they wouldn't have come without something to kill them.  He raced back to the clearing, where Oberon and his party were still waiting.

Brion said that they were quite surprised to actually see him again.  I don't know what he promised him, but Brion said that he was allowed to borrow Oberon's sword to slay the werewolves, and then had to return it.  I know this took but moments, but it was moments that I had to be fighting in a frenzy to cover the absence of one of us.  When Brion returned, he immediately felled one of the monsters from the side before they knew he was there.  It was then but a short time before they had all been slain, and we were left standing victors over the fallen.  Carrying our sorely wounded lord, we returned to the clearing and Oberon.  We thanked him for the aid he had given us in our battle against our mutual enemy, and wished him well on his journeys for we would probably not meet again.  But he looked at Brion and myself, smiled, and said to not wager too much on that.

As we returned to Chalke Castle, we talked on what we knew of werewolves, and found no one knew much.  But Hywel seemed positive that one of the ways a victim of a werewolf bite could be cured was for the werewolf to be slain before the next full moon.  On our arrival, we were feted once again by the beautiful Lady Jeanne and the Lady herself saw to Sir Gondarins wounds.  We spoke to her about the passing on of the werewolf curse to Sir Gondarins, and she looked at Hywel directly and said that it was certainly possible.  Through the night, we talked between ourselves, and with the Lady Jeanne, about the situation.  We would send word to Earl Robert about the werewolves' presence and elimination, and the possibility of the curse having been passed onto his son, as well as reporting on the rest of the patrol.  We believed we had saved him from the curse, but we suggested that perhaps we should all spend some time in Chalke Castle, until after the next full moon, at least.  Looking back, I know that I had more than my duty on my mind with my suggestions.

And so we stayed there nigh on a month, and I set to wooing the Lady Jeanne.  I fear that she was put out at the beginning, but I know that on the night I courted her with my harp and played as well as a mortal can, I captured her heart with my music so that she saw past my outward seeming.  I can still remember the moment …

Sorry, brother, forgive me for my distraction.  You're too kind.  Well, too sum it all up, we spent an enjoyable time together until the full moon approached and then Sir Gondarins had to suffer the indignity of nightly stays in the castle dungeon just in case he was still cursed.  As you can probably guess, he was cured?!  Is that the right word, I don't know!  Anyway, on our return to Sarum, our duties as companions to Sir Gondarins were ended, as he was being sent to take command of Vagon Castle, as they had been failing to provide their seasonal tithing and he was in need of stewarding training.

## Courting

For the rest of us, well, I returned to Broadchalke to court the beautiful and enchanting Lady Jeanne.  It felt like just a single day before I had plighted my troth to my beloved, but I know that some length of time must have passed.  Then again, Jeanne was never one to wait on what she wanted.  You blush again, brother.

Oh, the others … well, Brion was at a loss at court until he decided to set his sights on _[[030-Arthurian-Britain#^5c5164|Lady Anne of Longcott]]_, a lady of tempestuous and passionate nature.  To the surprise of all at the court, they wed that winter.  Edar was still cutting a swath amongst the ladies, but he still seemed almost quiet around them.  But there is more to that story later.

In fact, brother, it is late.  Let us call an end to this now, and an end for a while.  I must return to Jagent tomorrow and shall not return for a few weeks, at least.  Stay well with your God, and I will speed with mine own.
